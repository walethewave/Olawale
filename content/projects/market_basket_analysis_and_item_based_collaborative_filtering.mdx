# Market Basket Analysis & Collaborative Filtering

This project runs a UK online retailer's transaction history (806K+ transactions, ~5,900 customers, Dec 2009–Dec 2011, UCI's Online Retail II dataset) through RFM segmentation, K-Means clustering, and two different "what goes with what" techniques — Apriori market basket analysis and item-based collaborative filtering — deliberately scoped to the lowest-value customer tier.

## What It Does

Segments customers by Recency/Frequency/Monetary value, clusters them to validate that segmentation with an independent method, then focuses the recommendation work specifically on the Bronze (lowest-value) segment — the group with the most room to move, rather than re-analyzing the whole customer base at once.

## RFM Segmentation & Clustering (Verified Results)

- **Bronze:** 1,752 customers, avg 16.6 purchases, avg spend £233
- **Silver:** 2,382 customers, avg 65.0 purchases, avg spend £905
- **Gold:** 1,744 customers, avg 341.5 purchases, avg spend £5,330 — a ~23x spend gap between Gold and Bronze
- **K-Means (k=4, selected via elbow method)** clustered on the same RFM features, used to cross-check the RFM tiers with an independent, unsupervised method rather than trusting a single segmentation approach

## Market Basket Analysis: A Real Negative Result

Ran Apriori on the Bronze segment's purchase baskets, then filtered for strong rules (lift ≥ 6, confidence ≥ 0.70). The filtered result was an **empty set** — no product pairs in the Bronze segment met that bar. That's a real, reportable finding, not a failure to hide: it means Bronze customers' purchases don't cluster into a small number of dominant "always bought together" pairs at a strict threshold, which itself is useful — it argues against betting a merchandising strategy on a handful of blockbuster bundles for this segment, and toward either a lower threshold or a different technique (which is exactly why item-based collaborative filtering was tried next).

## Item-Based Collaborative Filtering (Bronze Segment)

Built a product co-occurrence matrix from Bronze-segment invoices, then computed Pearson correlation between products' purchase patterns to recommend items similar to what a customer already bought.

**A real limitation worth naming:** with sparse co-purchase data, many product pairs only overlap on a handful of invoices, which makes Pearson correlation unstable — several results land on exact values like 1.0, -0.5, or -0.577, the tell-tale signature of a correlation computed on very few shared observations rather than a robust signal. This is a legitimate limitation of correlation-based similarity on sparse retail data, not a bug: the technique works, but its confidence is only as good as the amount of co-purchase evidence behind each product pair.

## Solution Architecture

```
Raw transactions (Online Retail II, 806K+ rows)
  → Cleaning (drop missing Customer ID, remove cancelled/negative-quantity orders)
  → Outlier capping (IQR-based clipping on Price)
  → RFM computation (Recency, Frequency, Monetary per customer)
      → Quartile-based segmentation → Gold / Silver / Bronze tiers
  → K-Means clustering (k=4, elbow method) on scaled RFM features
      → cross-validates the RFM tiers independently
  → Scope down to Bronze segment specifically
      → Apriori (min_support=0.03) → association_rules (lift, confidence)
          → strict threshold (lift≥6, confidence≥0.70) → empty result (real finding)
      → Item-based collaborative filtering (co-occurrence matrix → Pearson correlation)
          → per-customer product recommendations
```

**Key engineering decisions:**

- **Two independent segmentation methods, not one** — RFM (rule-based, quartiles) and K-Means (unsupervised clustering) were both run on the same features. Getting comparable segment structure from two different methods is a basic sanity check that a single method's output isn't an artifact of its own assumptions.
- **Scoped the recommendation work to one segment, not the whole customer base** — Bronze customers were chosen deliberately as the segment with the most upside (low frequency, low spend, but still active), rather than running market basket analysis across a customer base that mixes fundamentally different buying behaviors.
- **Reported the empty result instead of loosening the threshold until something appeared** — it would have been easy to lower the lift/confidence bar until *some* rules showed up and present those as "the findings." Keeping the strict threshold and reporting zero qualifying rules is the more honest read of what the data actually supports.
- **Named the correlation instability rather than presenting the recommendations as more confident than they are** — exact ±1.0/-0.5 correlations on thin data are a known artifact, and calling that out matters for anyone deciding whether to trust a given product recommendation.

## What's Next

- Re-run Apriori on the Bronze segment with a lower confidence/lift threshold to see what weaker-but-real associations exist, rather than only the strict-threshold search
- Weight or filter the collaborative-filtering correlations by the number of co-occurring invoices, so thin-evidence pairs don't carry the same apparent confidence as well-supported ones
- Extend the same Bronze-first approach to Silver and Gold segments for comparison
